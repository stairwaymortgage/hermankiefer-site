## TRAILER — Do Right and Fear No One

In 1849, a twenty-three-year-old doctor stepped off a ship in New York with a sword, a medical diploma, and a small bag of gold coins.

A year earlier, he had helped lead a revolution. Now he was a wanted man. The Church had excommunicated him.

His name was Herman Kiefer. He's my great-great-great-grandfather.

He came to Detroit with almost nothing. He slept on a couch in a drugstore and opened his practice. He helped found the Republican Party under the oaks at Jackson, and he campaigned for Abraham Lincoln. He became a U.S. consul, and a Regent of the University of Michigan who helped build its medical school.

He married a woman so stubborn she refused to sign away their house — and that house became the Kiefer Building.

They raised six children. One son fought eleven duels. One painted a picture that won a prize at the Paris Salon. And one, Detroit's health officer, built a hospital to fight tuberculosis — and refused to let them name it after him. He named it for his father.

For a hundred years, that's all most people knew. A name on a hospital.

In 1951, his grandson wrote down the whole story and left blank pages at the back, for the next generation to fill.

This is that next volume. And if you're a Kiefer — whatever your last name is — it's your story too.

## EPISODE 1 ★ — The Book in the Attic

In 1951, a retired paper executive in Port Huron, Michigan finished a book he'd been working on for twenty-five years. Most of it he wrote in a single month, on a ship crossing the Mediterranean.

His name was Edgar Kiefer. He was the eldest grandson of Dr. Herman Kiefer. And he called his book *The Kiefer Saga.*

Edgar had grown up on the old doctor's stories. He'd watched the family scatter — to Chicago and New York, to Paris, to China, to Sweden. He wanted it all in one place before the people who remembered were gone.

On the first page he put the family motto: *Thue recht und scheue niemand.* Do right and fear no one.

And at the back of the book, he did something I can't stop thinking about. He left blank pages. He wrote that he hoped each family would keep its own record, "so that another volume can be added in fifty or a hundred years."

He even printed everyone's address — so the cousins could find each other.

Seventy-five years later, a copy turned up in a library in Fort Wayne, Indiana. I read every page.

My name is Jim Blackburn. Dr. Herman Kiefer is my great-great-great-grandfather.

And this is the next volume.

## EPISODE 3 ★ — The Mystery of 1825

Herman Kiefer was born on the nineteenth of November, 1825, in Sulzburg — a little town at the edge of the Black Forest, in the German state of Baden.

The family book says he was the only child of a doctor named Conrad Kiefer and his wife, Christine. His death certificate says the same.

But there's another record. In the church register at Sulzburg, there's a baptism from that year — a baby boy named Johann Ludwig Grether. His mother was a young unmarried woman named Rosina Grether. And the record says the child was later recognized by Dr. Conrad Kiefer.

Two records. Two mothers. Two hundred years later, we still don't know the whole story. And I'm not going to guess.

What we do know is what he became. He studied medicine at Freiburg, at Heidelberg, in Prague and in Vienna. He joined a student dueling corps, and he helped found another.

And the family tells one more story. One night, on a dare from his fellow students, young Herman let them put him under chloroform — a brand-new, untested drug. The Saga says he was the first person at Heidelberg ever to try it.

He woke up. Which, given everything that came next, is a good thing for all of us.

## EPISODE 4 ★ — 1848

In 1848, revolution swept across Europe. Kings were shaken from Paris to Vienna. And in the German state of Baden, one of the young men in the middle of it was a medical student named Herman Kiefer.

He was twenty-two. He was a delegate to the great people's assemblies at Offenburg. And in March of 1848, he chaired the people's convention at Freiburg.

The family book records what they were fighting for. A free press. Trial by jury. Freedom of religion. The separation of church and state. Protection from being arrested without cause.

Think about that. Everything Americans had written into their Constitution sixty years earlier — in Baden, in 1848, it was revolutionary. It could cost you your career. Your freedom. Your life.

The first uprising failed. In 1849 it flared again, and according to the Saga, Herman Kiefer was named one of a provisional government of three.

That May, in the middle of a revolution, he sat his final medical examinations — and passed.

And then he went to war.

## EPISODE 5 ★ — Rats in a Trap

In June of 1849, Dr. Herman Kiefer marched as a surgeon with the revolutionary army of Baden.

He was at Philippsburg on the twentieth of June. At Ubstadt on the twenty-third. And every day, the Prussian army pushed them back.

When the rebel commanders decided to retreat into the fortress of Rastatt, Kiefer argued against it. He said he would not be "shut up like rats in a trap."

He was right. Rastatt was surrounded. It fell. And many of the men inside were executed.

Herman Kiefer escaped across the Rhine to Strasbourg. The family says the Church excommunicated him. He could never safely go home again.

On the eighteenth of August, 1849, he sailed for America. He landed in New York on the nineteenth of September. He meant to go on to St. Louis — but he stopped in Detroit.

And on the nineteenth of October, 1849, he opened his medical office there.

He arrived with his sword, his diploma, and a small bag of gold coins. For his first months in America, he slept on a couch in a drugstore on Gratiot Avenue.

He never left.

## EPISODE 6 ★ — The Stubborn Wife

Her name was Franciska Kehle. She was born in 1826 in the Black Forest town of Bonndorf. In 1850, Herman's father escorted her across the ocean to Detroit — and that July, she married his son.

In America, she became Frances.

They started out in a little house at Hastings and Congress. Later they built a big home with a tower, and over the door they carved: *So ist's halt jetzt.* That's just how it is now.

The family remembered her as the steady one. When the doctor was in one of his moods, she'd tell the children, "The old bear is in his den, growling."

And then, in 1883, came the moment that changed the family's fortunes.

The doctor wanted to sell their property at the corner of Library and Gratiot. He needed his wife's signature. Frances said no.

She told the buyer: "Tell him you have a stubborn wife!"

They kept the land. Years later, the family built the Kiefer Building on that corner and leased it to the Crowley Milner department store. By 1951, it had earned them more than two million dollars.

My great-great-great-grandmother. Stubborn. And right.

## EPISODE 7 ★ — Lincoln's Man

The refugee who had fought for a free press didn't waste any time in his new country.

In 1854, a new political party was born at an outdoor meeting under the oak trees in Jackson, Michigan. It was called the Republican Party. And according to the family, Dr. Herman Kiefer was one of its organizers.

He became the leader of Michigan's German Republicans. He campaigned for John C. Frémont in 1856. And in 1860, he campaigned for a lawyer from Illinois named Abraham Lincoln — and brought Detroit's German voters with him.

Then came the Civil War.

Dr. Kiefer cared for the wounded. And the war came home to his family. A brother-in-law, Lieutenant Bruno Mauch of the First Michigan Infantry, was wounded at Bull Run, captured, and died in Libby Prison in Richmond. His revolver was passed down in the family.

When the war ended, Dr. Kiefer helped bring Detroit's great Soldiers' and Sailors' Monument to life — negotiating the casting of its bronze in Germany.

The man who fled one country for its ideals had found another worth fighting for.

## EPISODE 13 ★ — The Artist

Of all of Herman Kiefer's children, only one was born outside Detroit. Edwin Hans Kiefer was born in Port Huron in 1860 — and he was named for Hans Christian Andersen.

Maybe that's why he became the artist.

He started out drawing for a lithography company in Detroit. He moved to New York and painted watercolors for magazine covers. And then he went to Paris to learn to paint in oils.

He set up a studio in Étaples, a fishing village on the French coast where painters from all over the world had gathered. And there, he painted a young local woman named Marie Aubert, in a red blouse, reading a letter.

He called it *The Letter.* And according to the family book, it won a prize at the Paris Salon of 1890 — the most important art exhibition in the world.

Edwin painted the life-size portrait of his father that hung in the University of Michigan's medical school. He painted his mother too.

And in 1912, he married Marie Aubert. The woman in the painting.

Today, nobody in the family knows where *The Letter* is. If you do — please, tell me.

## EPISODE 16 ★ — The Name on the Hospital

The youngest of Herman Kiefer's children was named for the president. Guy Lincoln Kiefer.

He became a doctor like his father. And in 1901, he became Detroit's Health Officer.

Guy built the city a modern health department. Doctors in the schools. Nurses. Clinics for mothers and babies. He went after unsafe milk — and when a dairyman took him to court, Guy won.

Detroit's old "pest house" for contagious diseases was little more than a few shacks on the edge of town. Guy turned it into a real hospital — one of the early sanitariums for tuberculosis in America. His wife, Josephine — Aunt Jo to the whole family — spent weeks with a friend, scrubbing and fixing it up with their own hands.

When it was finished, the city wanted to name it after him.

In 1949, Aunt Jo told the family exactly what happened:

"They wanted to call it the Guy Kiefer Hospital. But Guy wouldn't have it — and got them to change it to the Herman Kiefer Hospital, after his father."

For more than a hundred years, Detroiters have said that name.

Now you know why.

## EPISODE 17 ★ — A Broken Heart

In August of 1909, Frances Kiefer died. She and Herman had been married for fifty-nine years.

That November, their eldest son, Alfred, fell from a Detroit streetcar and was killed.

The old doctor kept going. In the summer of 1910, at eighty-four years old, he made one more trip to Germany — back to Heidelberg, where his old student corps was celebrating its hundredth anniversary. The young revolutionary who had fled in 1849 came back as an honored guest.

Back in Detroit, the Kiefer Building was rising on the corner Frances had refused to sell.

He spent his last year in the home of his daughter, Hermina. And on the eleventh of October, 1911, Dr. Herman Kiefer died. He was eighty-five.

The family said he died of a broken heart.

That same year, on the north side of Detroit, a new city hospital opened its doors. It carried his name — because his son wouldn't let it carry his own.

Sixty-two years in America. Nine children. A city changed.

And a name that outlived all of them.

## EPISODE 20 ★ — Volume Two

When Edgar Kiefer finished the family book in 1951, he knew where everyone lived. He printed their addresses so they could write to each other.

Seventy-five years later, most of us have never met.

Herman Kiefer's descendants are out there — Kiefers, but also Danzigers and Parkers. Orrs, and Nilssons in Sweden. Joneses and Marrs and Bonnings. Lawries, Owens and Baylisses. Heyms, Bajises and Gilberts. And Blackburns, like me.

Some of you have a photograph in a shoebox that nobody's looked at in years. A letter. A painting. A story your grandmother used to tell at Thanksgiving. Maybe even an old snuff box, or a pair of dueling pistols, in an attic somewhere.

I built HermanKiefer.com so all of it has a place to go. You can add to the family tree, correct what we got wrong, share a photo or a memory — or just say hello.

My great-great-great-grandfather crossed an ocean with a sword, a diploma, and a small bag of gold coins. His grandson left us blank pages.

Let's fill them in. Together.

I'm Jim Blackburn. This is Volume Two.
